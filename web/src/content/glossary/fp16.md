FP16 (IEEE 754 **binary16**, also called half precision or `float16`) is a 16-bit floating-point format with $1$ sign bit, $5$ exponent bits (bias $15$), and $10$ fraction bits. It uses half the storage bytes per element of FP32 while keeping more mantissa precision than BF16. The cost is a **narrow dynamic range**: finite magnitudes top out at $65504$, and the smallest positive *normal* is about $6.1 \times 10^{-5}$. Smaller magnitudes can be subnormal, with reduced precision, before rounding or flushing to zero. Large values can overflow. This is why FP16 training recipes need [loss scaling](glossary://loss-scaling) and careful mixed-precision paths.

In ML and LLM systems, FP16 is both a **compute** type (Tensor Core matmuls that multiply FP16 inputs and often accumulate in FP32) and a **storage** type (weights, activations, gradients, and the [KV cache](glossary://kv-cache) at two bytes per element). It is not the same thing as [BF16](glossary://bf16): same bit width, different exponent/mantissa trade-off, different failure modes.

## Bit layout

| Format | Bits | Sign | Exponent | Fraction | Approx. max finite | Min positive normal |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FP32 | 32 | 1 | 8 (bias 127) | 23 | $\approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$ | $\approx 1.18 \times 10^{-38}$ |
| BF16 | 16 | 1 | 8 (bias 127) | 7 | $\approx 3.39 \times 10^{38}$ | $\approx 1.18 \times 10^{-38}$ |
| FP16 | 16 | 1 | 5 (bias 15) | 10 | $65504$ | $2^{-14} \approx 6.10 \times 10^{-5}$ |

Normalized values (stored exponent $E \in [1, 30]$) decode as:

$$
(-1)^{s} \times 2^{E-15} \times \bigl(1 + m \cdot 2^{-10}\bigr)
$$

where $m$ is the 10-bit fraction interpreted as an integer. Special encodings: $E = 0$ is zero or subnormals; $E = 31$ is $\pm\infty$ or NaN. The largest finite value is $(2 - 2^{-10}) \times 2^{15} = 65504$. The tiniest positive subnormal is $2^{-24} \approx 5.96 \times 10^{-8}$. Relative precision is about $2^{-10}$ near $1$ (roughly three decimal digits), better than BF16's $2^{-7}$ but far coarser than FP32's $2^{-23}$.

Around $1$, consecutive normals are $2^{-10} \approx 0.0009765625$ apart. Concrete rounds: $\pi$ becomes $3.140625$ and $1/3$ about $0.33325195$. This spacing is often adequate for matmuls when products accumulate in a wider type; tiny gradients can fall into subnormals or flush to zero on many GPU paths, and large intermediates can exceed $65504$.

## Intuition

Think of FP16 as "**half the bytes, more precision than BF16, much less range than FP32**." BF16 keeps FP32's exponent field and truncates the mantissa. FP16 keeps more fraction bits and steals them from the exponent. Neural nets often tolerate coarse mantissas; they do **not** tolerate activations or gradients that flush to zero or explode to `Inf` because the exponent range ran out.

That asymmetry is why Micikevicius et al. (2018) made three techniques standard for FP16 [mixed precision](glossary://mixed-precision) training:

1. **FP32 master weights** for the optimizer update (FP16 weights are only a working copy for forward/backward).
2. **Loss scaling** so small gradients occupy more of the FP16 range during backward.
3. **FP16 multiplies with FP32 accumulate** inside GEMMs (the Tensor Core pattern), then store results back to FP16 when needed.

Without those, "just cast everything to half" often fails even when the same model is fine in BF16.

## Example

**Memory.** A $7$B-parameter weight tensor alone is about $28$ GB in FP32 and about $14$ GB in FP16 ($7 \times 10^9 \times 4$ vs $\times 2$ bytes). Activations and the KV cache scale the same way: half the bytes per element, so larger batch size or longer context can fit in the same HBM.

**A gradient that dies without scaling.** A true gradient component of $1 \times 10^{-5}$ is a normal number in FP32/BF16. In FP16 it sits **below** the min normal ($\approx 6.1 \times 10^{-5}$): still representable only as a *subnormal* (reduced precision), and many GPU kernels flush subnormals to zero. Without loss scaling, a large share of small gradients vanish or lose bits; after enough steps, training stalls or diverges. Scale the loss by $S = 1024$ before backward and the same gradient becomes $1.024 \times 10^{-2}$, a comfortable normal. Unscale after backward so the optimizer still sees the intended update size.

**PyTorch AMP (FP16).** Autocast picks dtypes per op; `GradScaler` implements dynamic loss scaling:

```python
scaler = torch.amp.GradScaler("cuda")
for inputs, labels in loader:
    optimizer.zero_grad(set_to_none=True)
    with torch.autocast(device_type="cuda", dtype=torch.float16):
        logits = model(inputs)
        loss = criterion(logits, labels)
    scaler.scale(loss).backward()
    scaler.unscale_(optimizer)  # if you clip, do it after unscale
    torch.nn.utils.clip_grad_norm_(model.parameters(), max_norm)
    scaler.step(optimizer)
    scaler.update()
```

If any gradient is non-finite, the scaler skips the step and lowers $S$. BF16 autocast usually omits the scaler because range, not precision, was the main FP16 training hazard.

**Serving.** Inference engines often load weights as FP16 (`torch.float16` / vLLM `--dtype half` or `float16`). Current [vLLM dtype rules](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/configuration/engine_args/) use FP16 for FP32/FP16 checkpoints under `auto` and BF16 for BF16 checkpoints. Casting between BF16 and FP16 can change rounding; casting a BF16-trained model to FP16 can also overflow its narrower range and cause non-finite values. Treat dtype as part of the serving contract: verify successful loading, then check evals for the deployed cast.

## How it works in training

Typical FP16 mixed-precision step:

1. Keep **master weights** (and usually optimizer state) in FP32.
2. Cast weights and activations to FP16 for large matmuls and convolutions.
3. Accumulate products in FP32 inside the kernel when hardware supports it.
4. Store activations/gradients as FP16 to save bandwidth when the path is memory-bound.
5. **Scale the loss**, run backward, **unscale** gradients, optionally clip, then update master weights in FP32.
6. On `Inf`/`NaN` gradients, skip the update and reduce the scale (dynamic scaling).

Reductions (softmax, layer norm / batch norm stats) and some pointwise paths often stay in FP32 even when tensors in memory are FP16. That is intentional: arithmetic cost there is small compared with GEMMs, and full-precision accumulation avoids silent quality loss.

## Trade-offs

| | FP16 | BF16 |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamic range | Narrow; easy overflow/underflow | Matches FP32 |
| Mantissa precision | $10$ bits | $7$ bits |
| Loss scaling | Often required in training | Usually unnecessary |
| Failure mode | Range + scaler bugs (`Inf`/`NaN`, underflow) | Rounding / precision loss |
| Hardware | Broad half-precision GPU support (Volta+ Tensor Cores, etc.) | TPUs, Ampere+ GPUs, many modern CPUs |

FP16 can still be the right choice when the stack or kernels expect half (some quantized GEMMs historically preferred FP16), when you need slightly more mantissa than BF16, or when the GPU generation is strong at FP16 Tensor Core paths. Prefer BF16 on supported hardware when training instability is dominated by range. Neither replaces lower-bit [quantization](glossary://quantization) for aggressive inference compression; FP16 is full 16-bit float, not int8/int4.

Production failures usually look like: loss goes `NaN` after a few steps (scale too high or a kernel overflowed), training flatlines (scale too low or gradients underflowing), or serve metrics drift after a silent dtype cast. Compare a short FP32 or BF16 baseline, log gradient norms and skipped-step counts, and verify that sensitive layers actually run the precision you think they do.

## Why it matters here

FP16 is the classic half-precision format in GPU training and a default storage dtype for many open checkpoints and inference engines. It teaches the real mixed-precision trade-off: half the bytes and faster matmuls only count if range and scaling stay under control. Once you can explain why a $10^{-5}$ gradient falls out of the FP16 normal range (and often dies on GPU paths) but stays fine in BF16, and why master weights stay in FP32, you can debug AMP, choose dtypes for serve, and size activation/KV memory without treating "half precision" as one format.
